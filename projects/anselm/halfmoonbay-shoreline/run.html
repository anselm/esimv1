This is one run of the shoreline south of Pillar Point Harbor at Half Moon Bay, from 1961, the year the Army Corps of Engineers finished the breakwater. Waves move sand along the beach, the breakwater shelters some of it and starves the rest, and the bluff retreats where the beach runs out. Drag the bar to move through the years.

The beach is a single line that moves in and out. Where more sand arrives than leaves, the line moves seaward; where less, it retreats. The map shows the change since the start, and the charts follow four named places.

Sand moves along the shore with the waves. Some leaks through the breakwater and settles in the harbor, which is the silting the dredging question is about.

Two numbers from the Army Corps of Engineers' study can be compared with the as-built run: the rate sand enters the harbor and the rate the beach near the breakwater has eroded. A ratio of 1 is agreement.

Three of the model's constants were fitted: the share of sand that leaks through the breakwater, a correction to the wave direction, and the transport coefficient, which came out 80 times below the textbook value. The harbor ratio is close partly for that reason, and the erosion near the breakwater is several times too large.

  • The waves are one half-degree cell of a reanalysis, far offshore of the real surf zone.
  • A one-line model has no cross-shore shape, no rocks and no river sand.
  • The coast it starts from is today's OpenStreetMap line, not a 1961 survey.
  • Three constants are fitted, so agreement with the harbor record is not an independent check.
