# A fire run again on its own ground

On 7 January 2025 at 10:30 in the morning a fire started in the hills above Pacific
Palisades, Los Angeles, in a Santa Ana wind. It burned 23,448 acres, destroyed 6,845
buildings and killed twelve people before it was contained on 31 January (CAL FIRE's
incident record). This project runs that fire again on the real terrain, with the real land
cover and the hour's wind from a weather reanalysis, lit where and when the record says,
with nobody fighting it. Then it holds the result against the perimeters the incident's
own mapping teams drew as the fire grew. Every number on this page is read from the runs
above it; the prose was written on 2026-11-03 and revised on the 4th, and the runs may have
changed since.

## The fire

The record we hold is copied into the project's event folder, publisher by publisher, and
this is what it says. On New Year's Day a small fire, the Lachman fire, burned on the same
ridge and was declared out; crews reported smouldering ground and hot roots on 2 January and
left. Six days later, in the strongest Santa Ana wind the basin had seen in years, the fire
came back from that ground. UL's Fire Safety Research Institute, which reconstructed the day
from dispatch and radio logs for the state, puts the weather at ignition at 47 miles an hour
sustained with gusts to 64 at the Malibu Hills station, and the first hour like this: at
10:33 the fire was backing into Temescal Canyon; by 10:49 it was below the old burn scar and
pushing toward Palisades Drive; by 11:25 it had crossed the road; houses on Lachman Lane were
burning from 11:27; and within the first hour it had run over a mile south toward the ocean.

CAL FIRE's status reports give the growth as it was reported: 10 acres at 11:06, 200 at 11:31,
1,262 at 15:34, 2,921 just after midnight, 11,802 at 11:45 the next morning and 15,832 at 13:20
on 8 January, all local time. The incident's own maps are sparser. The first is a triangle drawn
by hand at 06:00 UTC on the 8th with 2,920 acres typed in; the first infrared perimeter is from
the following evening, 27 hours after ignition, at 15,824 acres; the fire was mapped at 19,629
acres a day later and 23,343 on the night of the 11th, and the final perimeter is 23,448. The
first night, when the fire went from the ridge to the sea and through the Palisades house by
house, has no map at all. The reconstructions of that night (UL's hourly figures) are pictures
in a report, not yet georeferenced here.

What people did is in the record too, with its limits. The incident's GIS archive holds 2,334
lines (554 kilometres of completed dozer line, 130 of hand line, 94 of road used as line) and
825 points (dozer pushes, drop points, helispots, division breaks, the command post at Will
Rogers), and every one carries the time the GIS feature was made rather than the time the work
was done; the first completed line is dated 19:40 UTC on 9 January, two days in. CAL FIRE's
reports count the resources: 149 engines, 4 dozers, 7 helicopters and 836 people on the
afternoon of the 8th, 476 engines, 48 dozers, 44 helicopters and 4,471 people by the 17th.
Reporting after the fire, as summarised to the author and not read here (the Los Angeles Times
of 13 January 2025, the state's analysis as covered by LAist, the Los Angeles Fire Department's
after-action review of October 2025), has the first day as a day on which nothing worked: the
aircraft grounded by the wind, the fuel breaks overrun, the crews turned from containment to
evacuation, and forty engines standing in repair yards. The second day, when the wind eased and
the record grew by only 3,800 acres, is the day the suppression record would have to explain,
and it is the day both models here get wrong.

Two projects run this fire. This one uses the 1972 spread formula every American fire
behaviour tool rests on, on a coarse reanalysis wind, as the baseline. The project beside it
([The Palisades fire, cell by cell](/anselm/palisades-cells)) is an engine of our own on the
same ground under NOAA's 3 km wind, with the buildings as bodies, the record's points and lines
drawn on the three-dimensional view at their times, and a first day that now matches the
infrared perimeter in pace and shape. The Eaton fire, which started the same evening across the
basin, is held back as the test for whatever is fitted here.

## The ground before the fire

The ridge the fire came back from has burned many times, and the record of that reaches a
long way down. Over the last 600 years the big fires of this coast have come at about the same
rate under every kind of management. Charcoal in the layered mud of the Santa Barbara Basin,
read by Mensing, Michaelsen and Byrne in 1999 for the years 1425 to 1985, holds at least twenty
fires of over 20,000 hectares. Every one came under Santa Ana conditions, most at the end of a
wet spell and the start of a drought. The average gap between them shows no change across the
Chumash period, the Spanish and ranching period, and the twentieth century with its fire
engines. The Park Service applies that reading to this range: large weather-driven fires "more
or less regularly" through "very different approaches to fire suppression." Further back the
nearest long record is Lake Elsinore, inland, where the mud shows almost no fire before 13,200
years ago and then ten times as much, holding for centuries, as a warm dry spell ended and people
arrived. The megafauna at La Brea were gone within about two hundred years of it (O'Keefe and
others, 2023). People lived in villages along this coast from about 8,000 years ago and burned
near homes and along trails; whether they set large fires on purpose is disputed between the
authors the county's foresters cite, and the Park Service says they did not. Nobody has read a
ten-thousand-year charcoal record from these mountains themselves.

What people changed is the ignitions, the fuel and the stakes. Lightning here comes in the wet
season, out of phase with the wind, and the county's own fire history (Radtke, Arndt and
Wakimoto, 1982) says plainly that "almost every fire in recorded history was accidentally or
deliberately set by man." Ranchers and homesteaders set them before 1900 in weather that kept
them small. After 1900 the hunters and the growing city set them in the fire season. From 1900
to 1918 the range burned over at least twice; the 1911 fire ran for weeks from Santa Monica into
Ventura County. Management changed three times. In 1793 the Spanish governor,
Arrillaga, banned burning anywhere it might do harm, California's first fire law. In 1919 Los
Angeles County stood up an organised fire force for the mountains, and the records begin. After
the 1961 Bel Air fire, which took 484 homes by embers landing on wood shake roofs, the city
built its brush-clearance programme and banned wood shingles on new roofs. Through all of that,
from 1919 to 1980, the coastal strip from Las Flores Canyon west burned three to five times, a
gap of 12 to 21 years, while inland of the ridge most ground burned once. The foresters saw a
cycle of about twenty years in the area burned. Suppression, they said, had worked inland and
not on the coast: "a steady fuel buildup and a shift from small to large disastrous fires." The
Park Service now puts the mean return interval at about 28 years and notes corridors burned
more than ten times since 1925. Keeley (2005) puts the rotation under fifteen years, "likely
many times shorter than the natural regime." He names too much fire, not too little, as the
threat to the chaparral: frequent burning turns it to grass, and grass burns more often again. The
fires on or beside this window are the 1938 Trippet Ranch fire into Mandeville Canyon, Bel Air
in 1961, Mandeville in 1978 through chaparral forty and sixty-three years old, Old Topanga in
1993 and Woolsey in 2018.

For the models here the point is the age of the ground. The foresters give the years after
which each cover carries a large fire again: ten for coastal sage, fifteen for south-slope
chaparral, twenty for north-slope. Our fuel is a land-cover class from 2021 with no age in it.
The time since the last fire, cell by cell, from the state's perimeter history, is the layer
this history asks for, and neither project has it yet. The sources and what each says are in
the devlog entry of 4 November 2026, *The deep history of the Palisades ground*; the two
papers are public Forest Service PDFs, the Park Service pages are its fire-frequency and
fire-history pages for the recreation area, and the Santa Barbara Basin paper is in
*Quaternary Research* 51.

## The actors

What decides the outcome, in the words a person at the fire would use, drawn from these
words. A solid arrow is a relationship at least one of the two projects models. A dashed one
is a relationship the world has and the models do not, which is where the second day went.

```esim
actors
ground: slope and aspect
weather: wind, dry air, heat, the sun
fuel: what grows there, how dry, how old
buildings: houses as fuel and as the stakes
people: crews, lines, aircraft, evacuation
fire
weather -> fire: drives the head downwind
weather -> fuel: dries it by the hour
ground -> fire: runs it uphill, slows it down
fuel -> fire: whether it carries, how hot, how long
fire -> fuel: resets its age for twenty years (not modelled)
fire -> buildings: lights them by embers and radiant heat
buildings -> fire: burn for an hour, throw embers of their own
people -> fire: cut lines, drop water (not placed in time)
fire -> people: outruns them on the first day (not modelled)
fire -> weather: makes its own wind (not modelled)
ground -> weather: bends the wind over the ridges (not modelled)
```

## What is in the runs

The ground is the Copernicus 30 m elevation model, from which the slope and aspect of
every cell come. The fuel is the European Space Agency's 10 m land-cover map, each 30 m
cell taking its most common class: shrubland becomes a chaparral fuel model, grassland a
grass model, tree cover a light timber-and-shrub model, built-up land a guessed rate the
model moves fire through houses at, and the sea does not burn. The wind, humidity and
temperature come hour by hour from the ERA5 reanalysis at one point in the middle of the
window, a 25 km average that reads a Santa Ana event mild: the reanalysis gives 6 to 8
metres a second with gusts to 16 on the first night, where news reports put the gusts on
the ridges at two and three times that (recalled from the reporting, not fetched).

Fire moves cell to cell by Rothermel's spread model, the one the United States has used in
its fire behaviour tools since 1972: a rate of spread from the fuel bed, the fuel's
moisture, the wind and the slope, shaped into an ellipse that points down the sum of the
wind and the slope. Nothing is tuned. The fuel parameters were written from memory of the
standard fuel models and have not yet been checked against the published tables; the page
on limits says so.

## What the record says, and what the model says

The incident's mapped perimeters are the measure. The first one, at 06:00 on 8 January
UTC, is a triangle drawn by hand with the acreage typed in (2,920 acres); the first real
perimeter is an infrared map from the following evening, 27 hours after ignition. Where the
paths stand at that hour, and at the end of the runs:

```esim
table 2025-01-08T21:25Z fire.burned_acres=burned obs.acres=mapped fit.sorensen=overlap fit.over_acres=burned_outside_the_map fit.missed_acres=mapped_but_unburned fire.wind_m_s=wind fire.gust_m_s=gust
```

```esim
table 2025-01-12T00:00Z fire.burned_acres=burned_by_the_end obs.acres=last_mapped fit.sorensen=overlap
```

On the reanalysis wind the model is slow. At the hour of the first infrared map it has
burned about an eighth of the mapped area, and it reaches the record's size only at the
end of the fourth day, when the real fire had been that size for three. The shape it ends
with is fair: a little over half of the burned-and-mapped union agrees. Four variants ask
whether the shortfall is the wind, the houses or the shrub. The reanalysis gusts in place
of the mean wind close part of the first day's gap and overrun by the end; the heavier
chaparral model burns less, not more; built-up land as a barrier changes little. None of
them produces the first night, when the real fire went from the ridge to the sea in hours.

Until the afternoon of 2026-11-03 these runs were made on broken ground. The elevation
window had a column of missing values every third or fourth cell, which the slope code
read as a cliff on either side, and the model then burned four times the record and filled
the window by the second day. The runs on this page are on the corrected ground; the
devlog entry *The holes in the ground* has the account.

## What that means

Two things are missing and they pull in opposite directions. The wind that blew on the
ridges that night was two or three times what a 25 km reanalysis says, and a spread model
on the real wind runs faster; the engine project beside this one, which reads NOAA's 3 km
analysis with its gusts, matches the first day's acreage. And nothing here is fought: by the
second day the real fire was being held on its eastern and northern flanks, so a model that
is slow early and unhindered late can end near the right size without having been right at
any hour. The hourly perimeters, not the final acreage, are the test. Three things are
known and are the next work:

- The wind shaped by the terrain, so the Santa Ana accelerates down the canyons and
  slackens in the lee, instead of one flat value for the window.
- The fuel table, recalled, to be checked line by line against the published models, and a
  fuel map made for fire beside the land-cover classes.
- Suppression as an agent: engines, dozers and aircraft placed by rule, holding lines
  where the record says lines held.

The arrival-time map on each run page shows where the model's fire went first.

## Not in these runs

Spotting (embers carried ahead of the front), crown fire, the fire's own weather, the
structures as fuel in any physical sense, evacuation, and the people. All of them are
on the list; none of them is modelled. The runs beneath carry every number's source, and
[Limits today](/docs/system/limits) says what is known to be wrong.
