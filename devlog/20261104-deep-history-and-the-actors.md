## The last 100 years: a 20-year cycle, set by the wind, and three changes of policy

Radtke, Arndt and Wakimoto, *Fire History of the Santa Monica Mountains* (USDA Forest Service
PSW-58, 1982; LA County Fire Department authors), is the paper for this range. Its facts:

- Before 1900 "most fires were started by local ranchers and homesteaders during weather
  conditions that prevented the development of large-scale fires." Lightning "is an almost
  unknown ignition source" here: it comes in the wet season, out of phase with the foehn winds.
  "Almost every fire in recorded history was accidentally or deliberately set by man."
- 1900 to 1918: "many large fires that burned the area on an average of at least two times":
  the 1903 Rindge, 1909 Malibu, 1910 Las Flores-Temescal, 1911 Santa Monica-Ventura (which
  burned "for several weeks and extended from Santa Monica into Ventura County"), 1913
  Topanga-Escondido. Hunters set fires in the fire season for the brush regrowth.
- 1919: Los Angeles County's Forestry Department established as the suppression agency for the
  unincorporated mountains; records begin. "The establishment of an organized fire fighting
  force in 1919 put an end to such fires."
- 1919 to 1980, fires over 100 acres: the coastal zone from Las Flores Canyon to the Ventura
  line "was burned 3-5 times giving an average burn frequency of from 12.4 to 20.7 years";
  small areas up to 7 times; "mountainous areas inland of the ridge line, for the most part,
  burned only once." Area burned per decade cycles with a period of "about 20 years."
- Suppression worked inland and not on the coast: "This resulted in a steady fuel buildup and
  a shift from small to large disastrous fires." Coastal sage carries a large fire again
  "within 10 years after a burn, south slope chaparral within 15 years, and north slope
  chaparral within 20 years." The 1978 Mandeville fire "wedged between the 1961 Topanga and
  Bel Air Fires and made a run in chaparral stands in excess of 40 and 63 years."
- Of the fires over 400 ha, 88 percent came after September; large fires run "from
  mid-September through December" on the Santa Ana.

The National Park Service's fire-frequency page for the recreation area (1925 to 2010): the
mean return interval "is currently about 28 years"; "a few places in the park have burned more
than 10 times since 1925", about every 8 years; intervals under about 20 years damage the
native shrubs and the park has "very little old growth chaparral." Keeley (USDA PSW-GTR-195,
2005): the Santa Monica Mountains "have a fire rotation interval of less than 15 years, and
this is likely many times shorter than the natural regime"; "fire suppression has not
effectively excluded fire" in southern California, and the threat to the shrublands is too much
fire, converting chaparral to grass that burns more often still.

Named fires on or beside this window (Wade Graham, "Malibu Burning", Huntington-USC Institute
on California and the West, 2025-01-09; acreages differ between lists): 1938 Trippet Ranch,
14,820 acres and 350 buildings, into Mandeville Canyon; 1961 Bel Air, 6,090 acres and 484
homes; 1970 Wright; 1978 Kanan and Mandeville; 1993 Old Topanga, 16,468 acres and 369 homes;
2018 Woolsey, 96,949 acres. Graham counts "at least 30 'significant' fires" in Malibu in the
ninety years before Woolsey.

Mitigation, as the sources give it, changed three times: 1793, Governor Arrillaga's
proclamation of 31 May, "California's first fire control law," banning burning "even at the
most remote distances" (Radtke; UC Santa Barbara's 2023 account of the Chumash cultural burn);
1850, statehood, fire control the landowner's business, and deliberate burning for grazing and
access increased; 1919, organised suppression; and after the 1961 Bel Air fire, which ran on
ember-lit wood shake roofs, the city's brush-clearance programme and a ban on wood shingle
roofs in new construction (Wikipedia's Bel Air Fire article, citing the LAFD Historical Society
and an LA Times piece of 1989-07-22, "Bradley Signs Shake Roof Ban"; I did not read either).

## The last 1,000 years: the same big fires under every regime

Mensing, Michaelsen and Byrne, *Quaternary Research* 51 (1999), read microscopic charcoal in
the varved sediments of the Santa Barbara Basin from AD 1425 to 1985: "a minimum of 20 large
fires" (over 20,000 ha) in the Santa Barbara region, "high accumulations correspond to large
fires ... that occurred during Santa Ana conditions," "large fires consistently occurring at the
end of wet periods and the beginning of droughts," and "the average time between fires shows
no distinct change across three different land use periods: the Chumash period, apparently
characterized by frequent burning; the Spanish/Early American period with nominal fire control;
and the 20th century with active fire suppression." Pollen says the same: no dramatic change
in 500 years. That basin is a hundred-odd kilometres up the coast under the same wind; the
Park Service applies it to this range: "large weather-driven fires have occurred more or less
regularly throughout the last 600 years," through "very different approaches to fire
suppression."

A lake record at Dune Pond, Santa Barbara County (Mensing and others, 2015, abstract only)
has fire "increased sharply at AD 1100" and reads the record as human, since natural ignitions
are rare. The Park Service's reading of the people here: villages from about 8,000 years ago,
fire used "to manage portions of the landscape for food and material production" near homes
and trails, and "they did not deliberately light large scale fires." Radtke cites Drucker 1937
for sparing use and Lewis 1973 for spring burning to keep grassland; these disagree, and
neither is for the Tongva side of the range specifically. I found nothing Tongva-specific.

## The last 10,000 years: fire arrives with people, at the end of a warm dry spell

The nearest long record is Lake Elsinore, inland of here. O'Keefe and others, *Science* 2023
(read through the LA Times account reprinted on phys.org, 2023-08-17): "Prior to 13,200 years
ago, the mud cores show minimal fire activity"; then "the charcoal abundance increases by an
order of magnitude, and then it remains high for the next few hundred years," at the end of a
warm dry phase of 14,000 to 13,000 years ago and as people appear, with the La Brea megafauna
gone within about 200 years. For the early Holocene the only record I reached is a stalagmite
in the Coast Range near Davenport (Homann and others, *Nature Communications* 2022, 8,600 to
6,900 years ago): fire and hydroclimate "whiplash" tightly coupled around the 8.2 ka event.
That is central California, not this range. There is no 10,000-year charcoal record for the
Santa Monica Mountains in what I found.

## What it says to the simulator

The regime at the scale of the fires we model is the wind and the drought, and it has not
moved under Chumash, Spanish, ranching or suppression management. What people changed is the
number of ignitions (every one of them, in a range with no lightning), the fuel (suppression
built it inland; frequent fire thinned and grassed the coast), and the stakes (the houses the
1961 fire taught the city to roof differently). So the ground has an age, and the paper says
the age sets whether a fire carries: 10 years for sage, 15 for south-slope chaparral, 20 for
north. Our fuel layer is a 2021 land-cover class with no age. A time-since-last-fire field
from the state's fire perimeter history would be a real layer, and it is what the Palisades
had after the 1938, 1961, 1978 and 1993 fires. Not fetched; the source to look up is CAL FIRE's
fire perimeter database.

## The diagram

The wiring on a run page draws the modules a manifest lists, from what each declares. For the
cells runs that is five boxes, two forward couplings (fire.burned_frac to the two scorers),
no feedbacks, and ten outputs nothing reads. The actors Anselm names are all inside one of the
boxes: fire.cells loads terrain, cover, buildings, roads and the hour's wind itself, as six
assets its describe lists, and the drawing shows none of them. The diagram is honest about the
manifest and silent about the world.

Two routes, not exclusive. The cheap one: draw the assets as source nodes, so the ground, the
cover, the buildings, the roads, the weather and the record appear as boxes with arrows into
whoever loads them. That is one evening in Wiring.svelte and makes the current monolith
visible as a monolith. The right one is what the suppression move began: the engine becomes a
reactor among field-owning modules, a ground module owning elevation and slope, a fuel module
owning kind, load, moisture and age (where the fire history would go), a weather module owning
wind, humidity and temperature by the hour (HRRR or ERA5 chosen there, not in the engine), an
insolation module (sun.irradiance exists for the Boone and the scene already computes the sun),
a structures module owning the bodies, suppression owning the breaks. Then the wiring draws
itself, each actor is swappable per fire, and Fort McMurray's three substitutions would have
been three manifest lines. Neither is started; this is the thought.
